[Heading 1] CHAPTER 1
INTRODUCTION
[Heading 2] 1.1  GENERAL
[Normal] Disasters such as floods, cyclones and earthquakes create an immediate need for trained manpower, medical aid and logistics. Government agencies cannot meet this demand alone, and NGOs and citizen volunteers carry a large share of the response. The Sendai Framework (UNDRR, 2015) explicitly recognises their role. The Disaster Volunteering Network (DVN) is a full-stack web platform that connects volunteers, NGOs and an administrator through role-based portals: NGOs publish tasks, volunteers apply, NGOs approve and verify the work done, and verified work is converted into hours, points and a badge.
[Heading 2] 1.2  PROBLEM STATEMENT
[Normal] Coordination between volunteers and NGOs is largely informal. There is no single place to publish and discover tasks, skills are not matched to needs, capacity is not controlled, attendance and hours cannot be verified, volunteers receive no verifiable record, and administrators have no visibility of platform activity.
[Heading 2] 1.3  OBJECTIVES
[List Paragraph] To provide secure registration and login for volunteers, NGOs and administrators, with administrator verification of NGOs.
[List Paragraph] To let NGOs create and manage tasks, and volunteers filter tasks by skill and apply without duplicates or over-subscription.
[List Paragraph] To provide approval, check-in and proof-of-work submission, with credit of hours, points and badge only after NGO verification.
[List Paragraph] To give administrators statistics, audit logs, user-status control and emergency broadcasts.
[List Paragraph] To automate build verification and deployment through CI/CD.
[Heading 2] 1.4  SCOPE AND ORGANISATION
[Normal] The system is a browser-based prototype covering the complete volunteering lifecycle for three roles. Native mobile apps, real-time push notifications and geo-location matching are left as future work. Chapter 2 reviews the literature, Chapter 3 analyses the system, Chapter 4 presents the design, Chapter 5 covers implementation and testing, and Chapter 6 gives the results and conclusion.
[Heading 1] CHAPTER 2
LITERATURE REVIEW
[Heading 2] 2.1  ICT AND VOLUNTEERS IN DISASTERS
[Normal] Haddow, Bullock and Coppola (2017) describe emergency management as a cycle of mitigation, preparedness, response and recovery in which coordination decides the quality of response. Palen and Liu (2007) argue that crisis management must plan for ICT-supported public participation. Whittaker, McLennan and Handmer (2015) find that spontaneous volunteers add capacity and local knowledge but pose problems of coordination, training and accountability, and that registration, skill-based role matching and recognition are key. Existing channels such as messaging groups offer no verification, capacity control or record keeping; generic volunteer tools lack the emergency context.

[Heading 2] 2.2  TECHNOLOGIES USED
[Normal] Node.js uses an event-driven, non-blocking I/O model (Tilkov and Vinoski, 2010), and Express.js provides routing and middleware. REST (Fielding, 2000) is an architectural style for exposing resources through HTTP. MongoDB stores document-oriented data (Chodorow, 2013; Banker et al., 2016), and Mongoose provides schemas and validation. JSON Web Tokens (Jones et al., 2015) can support stateless authentication when tokens are validated consistently on protected server routes. AngularJS (Green and Seshadri, 2013) is used for the NGO portal.
[Heading 2] 2.3  RESEARCH GAP
[Normal] Volunteers are valuable but poorly coordinated. The gap addressed here is a lightweight platform that combines NGO verification, skill-based discovery, an approval and check-in workflow, proof-based verification of hours and administrative monitoring on a common JavaScript stack.
[Heading 1] CHAPTER 3
SYSTEM ANALYSIS
[Heading 2] 3.1  EXISTING AND PROPOSED SYSTEM
[Normal] At present, tasks are announced by calls and messaging groups, attendance is on paper or absent, and hours are self-reported. The proposed DVN replaces this with three portals over one REST API and database: the volunteer portal (profile, skills, task filter, application, check-in, proof, history, reminders, badge), the NGO portal (dashboard, task management, request approval, proof verification, volunteer list) and the administrator portal (statistics, user verification and suspension, audit logs, emergency broadcast, highlights).
[Heading 2] 3.2  REQUIREMENTS
[Normal] Functional requirements:
[List Paragraph] Registration and login; NGOs remain “Pending Verification” until activated by the administrator, and suspended users cannot log in.
[List Paragraph] NGO task creation with skills, location, schedule and capacity; edit, publish, complete, cancel or delete.
[List Paragraph] Skill-based task discovery; one application per volunteer per task; rejection when capacity is full.
[List Paragraph] NGO approval or rejection of requests; check-in and proof (1–24 hours) only after approval, one proof per task.
[List Paragraph] NGO proof verification crediting hours and 20 points per hour; badge after at least one approved proof.
[List Paragraph] Administrator statistics, searchable audit log, user-status change and emergency broadcast.
[Normal] Non-functional requirements are usability, security (token sessions, role checks, server-side validation), consistency of counts and credited hours, non-blocking performance, modularity, portability and auditability through a system log.

[Heading 1] CHAPTER 4
SYSTEM DESIGN
[Heading 2] 4.1  SYSTEM ARCHITECTURE
[Normal] DVN follows a three-tier architecture (Figure 4.1). The browser portals talk to the server only through api.js, which attaches the JWT as a bearer token, while auth.js keeps the session and enforces role redirects. The Express server applies CORS, JSON parsing (15 MB limit for image proofs) and logging, then dispatches to seven routers; it also serves the frontend files. Mongoose models store data in the MongoDB database disaster_volunteering_network.
[Normal] Figure 4.1  Three-tier architecture of DVN
[Heading 2] 4.2  USE CASE MODEL
[Normal] The volunteer registers, manages the profile, applies, checks in, uploads proof and generates a badge. The NGO manages tasks, decides requests and verifies proofs. The administrator verifies users, views statistics and logs, and sends broadcasts (Figure 4.2).

[Heading 1] CHAPTER 5
IMPLEMENTATION AND TESTING
[Heading 2] 5.1  IMPLEMENTATION
[Normal] Backend. server.js loads environment variables, connects to MongoDB (MONGO_URI or a local default), mounts the routers under /api and exposes /api/health. Mongoose schemas enforce enumerations and limits such as proof hours of 1 to 24. At login, suspended users and unverified NGOs receive a 403 response and a JWT is returned otherwise; registrations and logins are written to the system log.
[Normal] Volunteer module. Applying checks that the task is Published, not full and not already applied for (409 on violation). Check-in and proof submission require an Approved request, and only one proof is accepted per task. The history route marks a task Completed once proof exists. The badge “Certified Disaster Volunteer Specialist” is generated only after an approved proof, with an ID such as DVN-A1B2C3.
[Normal] NGO and admin modules. NGOs operate only on tasks they created. Approving a request increments volunteersAssigned within capacity, and reversing it decrements the count. Approving a proof adds the hours and 20 points per hour to the volunteer, and reversal subtracts them. The NGO portal is an AngularJS single-page application with routed views, services and factories. The administrator can change user status (recorded as AUDIT entries), search the 100 latest log entries and dispatch broadcasts shown with an audio alert on the volunteer dashboard. A seed script (npm run seed) inserts demonstration users, tasks and data.
[Heading 2] 5.2  CI/CD PIPELINE
[Normal] A GitHub Actions workflow runs on pushes and pull requests to main. It sets up Node.js 20, installs dependencies with npm ci and checks server.js with node --check. Only on a successful push to main it calls a Render deploy hook stored as the secret RENDER_DEPLOY_HOOK, so credentials are never committed.

[Heading 2] 6.2  CONCLUSION AND LIMITATIONS
[Normal] DVN provides a prototype workflow for NGO task publishing, volunteer applications, approval, check-in, proof submission and NGO review, together with hour and point tracking, badge functionality and administrative features. The implementation has important limitations: passwords are stored without hashing; JWT checks are not consistently applied across API routes, and some routes rely on e-mail values supplied by the client; skill filtering is keyword-based and the displayed match rating is stored rather than dynamically calculated; proof images are stored in the database; and the CI workflow performs a server syntax check rather than a complete automated test suite. These limitations mean the application should not be described as production-secure.
[Heading 2] 6.3  FUTURE ENHANCEMENTS
